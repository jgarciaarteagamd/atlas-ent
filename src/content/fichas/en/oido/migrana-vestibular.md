**Vestibular migraine** is a form of migraine in which the main symptom is **dizziness or vertigo**, not always headache. It is one of the more common causes of repeated dizziness with no clear cause in the ear. It is diagnosed from what you tell us and treated with changes in habits and, sometimes, medication.

## What is vestibular migraine?

The **vestibular system** is your balance system: the inner ear and the brain pathways that connect to it. In vestibular migraine, the same mechanism that causes migraine affects that system. You get **attacks of dizziness or vertigo** lasting from **a few minutes up to three days**.

It is thought to affect fewer than 1 in 100 people, but many are never diagnosed. It is more common in women and in people who have or have had migraine.

## What are the symptoms?

- **Vertigo** (the feeling that everything is spinning), spontaneous or triggered by **head movement** or a change of position.
- **Dizziness** or unsteadiness, sometimes with **nausea** and discomfort with movement.
- **Sensitivity to light and noise** during an attack.
- **Headache** with migraine features, which may come with some attacks, not all.
- Seeing flashing lights or blind spots (**aura**) in some people.

A useful clue: there is **not usually major hearing loss** or constant ringing. If you have them, they may be due to another condition.

## Why does it happen?

The exact cause is not known. It is thought that the brain in people with migraine is **more sensitive** to certain stimuli, and that this changes how it handles balance signals. Attacks can be **triggered** by:

- Lack of or changes in **sleep**.
- **Stress** and anxiety.
- Skipping meals or long fasts.
- **Hormonal** changes, such as periods.
- **Bright lights**, noise, strong smells or weather changes.
- Some foods or **alcohol**, in some people.

Triggers are **different for each person**.

## How is it diagnosed?

Diagnosis is **clinical**: it relies on your history. There is no test that confirms it. The **key points** are:

- At least **5 episodes** of moderate or severe dizziness or vertigo lasting **5 minutes to 72 hours**.
- A history of **migraine**, current or past.
- In **at least half the attacks**, a migraine feature: typical headache, sensitivity to light or noise, or aura.
- That it is **not better explained** by another condition.

To rule out other causes we examine you and, depending on the case, arrange a [hearing test](/en/areas/ear/pure-tone-audiometry-hearing-test/) and balance tests such as videonystagmography. Other possible causes include [BPPV](/en/areas/ear/benign-paroxysmal-positional-vertigo/), Ménière's disease or a problem in the brain. Scans are not usually needed unless there are unusual symptoms.

## How is it treated?

The scientific evidence on treatment is **limited**: there are few studies and they are of low quality. What we do draws on experience with ordinary migraine.

### General measures

Understanding the condition, keeping an **attack diary** and looking after your habits (sleep, meals, exercise, stress) is the foundation. We explain it in [preventing vestibular migraine attacks](/en/areas/ear/vestibular-migraine-prevention/).

### During an attack

- Rest in a quiet, dimly lit place.
- Medicine for nausea or for migraine may be prescribed, depending on your case.
- Avoid taking **painkillers** every day, because they can make attacks more frequent.

### Preventive medication

If attacks are frequent or disabling, **preventive medicines** of the kind used for migraine can be tried. Reviews show that studies are few and small, so we cannot say which is best. The choice is individual.

### Vestibular rehabilitation

If you are left **unsteady** between attacks, exercises guided by a physiotherapist can help. There are few specific studies, but it is a safe option.

### Psychological support

Stress and anxiety make attacks worse, and **talking therapy** can help those who need it.

## Advice

- **Note down** each attack: date, duration, symptoms and what you did beforehand.
- **Sleep** regularly and **do not skip meals**.
- **Exercise** moderately and regularly.
- **Identify your triggers** without banning everything.
- **Do not overuse painkillers.**
- **Do not drive** or climb heights during an attack.
- **See us** if the pattern of your attacks changes.

## When should I seek urgent help?

Some dizziness can come from a problem in the brain. **Call 112 (in Spain) or your local emergency number, or go to A&E,** if the dizziness or vertigo:

- Comes with a **very severe, sudden headache**.
- Comes with **double vision**, **difficulty speaking**, **weakness or tingling** on one side of the body, or a drooping face.
- Stops you **standing**, even without moving your head.
- Comes with **sudden hearing loss**.
- Is the **first time** and you have vascular risk factors (high blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, older age).

**Book an appointment soon** if attacks are becoming more frequent or limit your daily life.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I have vestibular migraine without a headache?**
Yes. Many attacks occur with dizziness or vertigo alone. The diagnosis relies on migraine features in at least half the attacks.

**Is it serious?**
It does not damage the ear or the brain, but it can limit your life a lot. The important thing is to rule out other causes.

**Does it go away?**
It can usually be controlled with habits and, if needed, medication. Attacks can come back, sometimes with good and bad spells.

**Will I lose hearing?**
Normally not. If you do, other causes, such as Ménière's disease, need to be investigated.

**Is it the same as BPPV?**
No. BPPV lasts seconds and is triggered by particular positions. Vestibular migraine lasts minutes to hours.

**Do I have to give up chocolate and coffee?**
Not necessarily. Only if you have found that they trigger attacks for you.

**Can I exercise?**
Yes, moderately and regularly. Very intense exercise can trigger attacks in some people.
