A **nosebleed**, or **epistaxis**, is very common: according to the 2020 US guideline, at least 60 % of people have one at some point, and only about 6 % of them need medical attention. It almost always starts at the front of the nose and can be stopped at home with a simple technique. We explain how, and when to ask for help.

## What are these measures for?

Pinching the nose firmly presses on the bleeding vessels and lets a clot form. Leaning forward stops you swallowing blood, which causes nausea.

## Before you start

- Stay calm: most nosebleeds stop this way.
- Have tissues, a bowl and a clock to hand.
- Do not put anything inside your nose.

## How to do it

1. **Sit down** and **lean slightly forward.** Do not tilt your head back or lie down.
2. **Blow your nose gently once** to clear any clots.
3. **Pinch the soft part of your nose firmly,** just below the bone, closing both nostrils.
4. **Breathe through your mouth** and spit out any blood; do not swallow it.
5. **Keep up the pressure for at least 5 minutes without letting go,** watching the clock. Do not release to check.
6. If you wish, hold something cold wrapped in a cloth on your forehead or the back of your neck. It is a supporting measure and does not replace pressure.
7. Let go slowly. If it is still bleeding, pinch again for another 5 minutes or more.

## Routine

| Routine | What to do |
|---|---|
| **How often** | At every nosebleed |
| **For how long** | At least 5 minutes of continuous pressure; if it is still bleeding, repeat |
| **When to stop** | When the bleeding stops. If it has not stopped after about 20 minutes of correct pressure, go to A&E |

For the next 24-48 hours, avoid blowing your nose hard, straining and very hot drinks.

Always follow the routine your doctor gave you, which may be different.

## Common mistakes

- Tilting your head back or lying down.
- Pinching the hard part (the bone) instead of the soft part.
- Letting go every so often to look.
- Putting cotton wool or products into the nose, including hydrogen peroxide.
- Stopping your blood-thinning medication on your own.

## What is normal and when to seek advice

In the following days it is normal to see traces of blood or small scabs.

**Call 112 (in Spain) or your local emergency number, or go to A&E, if:**

- It has not stopped after about 20 minutes of correct pressure.
- The bleeding is heavy, or you feel dizzy, weak, pale or short of breath.
- You vomit a lot of blood.
- It follows a hard blow to the face or head.

**Book an appointment with us if:**

- The nosebleeds keep coming back.
- You take blood-thinning medicines and bleed often.
- You always bleed from the same side and also have a blocked nose.
- You bruise easily.

## Prevention

- Keep your nails short and avoid picking your nose.
- Blow your nose gently, one nostril at a time.
- If the air is dry, use a humidifier or do saline nasal rinses.
- If you get crusts, apply a little petroleum jelly just inside the nostrils.
- Avoid tobacco smoke and strong irritants.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it dangerous if I take blood thinners or aspirin?**
You will bleed more easily, but these measures still work. Do not stop your medication without talking to your doctor.

**Does high blood pressure cause nosebleeds?**
It has not been shown to cause them directly. If you feel unwell, seek advice.

**My child gets frequent nosebleeds. Is it serious?**
Usually not; it is often due to picking or dry air. See us if they keep coming back, are heavy or your child bruises easily.
