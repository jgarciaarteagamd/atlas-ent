**Salivary stones**, or **sialolithiasis**, are small calcified lumps that form inside a salivary gland or its duct and block it. They typically cause **swelling and pain under the jaw or in the cheek when you eat**, which settle after a while. They are one of the most common problems of the salivary glands.

## What are salivary stones?

We have several **salivary glands** that make saliva and carry it into the mouth through fine ducts. The main ones are the **parotid glands** (in the cheek, in front of the ears), the **submandibular glands** (under the jaw) and the **sublingual glands** (under the tongue).

Sometimes the calcium salts in saliva clump together and form a **stone** that blocks the duct. Saliva builds up behind it and the gland swells. The **submandibular gland** is the most often affected, and the parotid less so.

## What are the symptoms?

- **Swelling and pain** under the jaw or in the cheek that come on when you **eat, or even think about food**, and settle within an hour or two.
- A **hard lump** you can feel under the tongue or on the floor of the mouth.
- A **salty taste** or **pus** in the mouth.
- With infection: **constant pain, redness, warmth** and sometimes a **fever**.
- Episodes **recur** whenever you eat until the blockage clears.

## Why do they happen?

In many cases the cause is unknown. They may be encouraged by:

- **Dehydration** or **thick saliva**.
- Some medicines that **dry the mouth**.
- Previous **infections** or inflammation of the gland.
- A **narrow** or damaged duct.

## How are they diagnosed?

1. **History and examination:** we feel the gland and the floor of the mouth. Sometimes the stone can be felt with the fingers.
2. **Ultrasound:** the most widely used test. It is quick, painless, uses no radiation and usually shows the stone and the swollen gland.
3. **CT or MRI:** if ultrasound is not enough or there is doubt.
4. **Sialendoscopy:** a very fine camera passed along the duct lets us see and sometimes treat the stone at the same time.

## How are they treated?

Treatment depends on the size and position of the stone.

### Conservative treatment

For **small stones close to the opening of the duct**, conservative treatment often gives good results. It includes **drinking water, heat, massaging the gland** and stimulating saliva with sharp-tasting foods. It is all in [self-care for salivary stones](/en/areas/salivary-glands/salivary-stones-self-care/).

**Painkillers** help with the pain. **Antibiotics** are used only if there is infection.

### If the stone does not pass with these measures

When the stone is not cleared by conservative treatment, there are other options:

- **Sialendoscopy:** a very fine endoscope is passed along the duct. It allows the stone to be removed or broken up with a laser, preserving the gland.
- **Removal through the mouth:** if the stone is near the opening of the duct.
- **Other surgery** on the salivary ducts or glands, in specific cases.

The most suitable option depends on the size and position of the stone.

## Advice

- **Drink water** through the day.
- **Massage** the gland gently towards the mouth.
- **Keep your mouth clean:** brush your teeth and tongue.
- **Do not try to remove the stone** with needles or objects.
- **Do not ignore** repeated episodes: a lasting blockage can infect the gland.
- **Tell us** which medicines you take, in case one dries the mouth.

## When should I seek urgent help?

**Call 112 (in Spain) or your local emergency number, or go to A&E,** if:

- The **floor of the mouth or the neck** swells quickly and you have **difficulty swallowing or breathing**.
- You have a **high fever** with severe pain, redness and marked swelling.
- You cannot open your mouth normally.

**Book an appointment soon** if:

- The swelling **does not go down** after several hours or lasts more than **2 weeks**.
- Episodes **recur** or **pus** comes out.
- You notice a **lump in the neck or cheek** that **does not change with meals**.
- Your mouth is very dry.

## Frequently asked questions

**Are salivary stones serious?**
Not usually, but they can become infected. It is worth treating them if they keep recurring.

**Do they go away on their own?**
Small ones sometimes pass with water, massage and saliva stimulation. Larger ones usually need a procedure.

**Does sialendoscopy hurt?**
It is done under local or general anaesthetic, depending on the case. Afterwards there may be mild discomfort.

**Do I have to have the gland removed?**
Almost never. Nowadays the gland is preferably kept, and removed only in specific cases.

**Can they come back?**
Yes, in some people. Drinking water and good oral hygiene help.

**Is it the same as mumps?**
No. Mumps is a viral infection of the parotid gland and causes fever and swelling in both cheeks. With a stone, the swelling appears when you eat.

**Could it be a tumour?**
Almost always not. But a fixed lump that does not change with meals needs to be investigated.
