**Functional dysphonia** is a change in your voice, with hoarseness, tiredness or effort when speaking, that happens **without serious damage to the vocal cords**. The muscles of the voice box and neck are working with too much tension. It is very common in people who use their voice a lot, and it is mainly treated with **voice therapy**.

## What is functional dysphonia?

**Dysphonia** means "an altered voice": rougher, weaker, higher or lower than normal, or that tires easily. It is **functional** when the problem lies in **how the voice is used**, not in a significant lesion of the vocal cords.

It is also called **muscle tension dysphonia**, and it is the most common functional voice problem. In **functional aphonia**, the voice disappears altogether, although coughing or laughing still produces sound.

## What are the symptoms?

- A **hoarse** or rough voice that gets worse through the day.
- **Vocal fatigue:** talking is hard work and your voice cracks or fails by the evening.
- **Effort** when speaking, as if you have to push the voice out.
- **Tension or pain** in the throat or neck.
- A feeling of a **lump** in the throat and a need to clear it.

It often improves with rest or on holiday and gets worse with work.

## Why does it happen?

Usually several things happen at the same time:

- **Heavy or incorrect voice use:** teachers, call-centre staff, singers, or always talking in noisy places or by shouting.
- **Stress and muscle tension** in the neck and shoulders.
- An **infection** (for example, after a cold) after which you keep pushing the voice.
- **Reflux** and dryness can contribute but are rarely the only cause.
- **Smoking** irritates the vocal cords.

## How is it diagnosed?

1. **History:** when it began, how you use your voice, what makes it better or worse.
2. **[Flexible nasendoscopy](/en/areas/throat/flexible-nasendoscopy/):** a thin tube passed through the nose shows the vocal cords. It is **essential** to rule out nodules, polyps, paralysis or other lesions, and before starting voice therapy.
3. **Neck examination:** we look for muscle tension.
4. **Assessment by a speech and language therapist,** who looks at how you breathe, speak and project your voice.

Blood tests, CT or MRI are **not usually needed** before the larynx has been seen.

## How is it treated?

### Voice therapy with a speech and language therapist

This is the **main treatment**. It consists of sessions with a therapist to learn to breathe better, relax your neck and use your voice without straining. It includes exercises to do at home. Most people improve with it. **We refer you to a speech and language therapist** once your larynx has been examined.

### Voice care

Daily habits are part of the treatment. We explain them in [vocal hygiene: how to look after your voice](/en/areas/larynx/vocal-hygiene-voice-care/).

### Treat contributing factors

If there are signs of reflux, allergy or dryness, we treat them. Stopping smoking helps.

### What usually does not help

- **Antibiotics:** they do not work unless there is a bacterial infection.
- **Steroids and reflux medicines** for hoarseness alone, before the larynx has been seen: guidelines recommend **not** giving them.
- **Total voice rest** for a long time: it can increase tension.

## Advice

- **Do not strain your voice**, shout or talk over loud noise.
- **Take breaks** if you talk a lot, and sip water through the day.
- **Do not smoke** or breathe in smoke.
- **Avoid throat clearing:** swallow or take a sip of water instead.
- **Look after your neck and shoulders:** stretch and relax during the day.
- **Go to your therapy sessions** and practise at home.

## When should I seek urgent help?

**Call 112 (in Spain) or your local emergency number, or go to A&E,** if you have:

- **Difficulty breathing** or a high-pitched noise when you breathe.
- Trouble **swallowing** even saliva.
- **Blood** when you cough.

**Book an appointment soon** if:

- Hoarseness lasts **more than 4 weeks**.
- You smoke, drink alcohol often or are over 45.
- You notice a **lump in the neck**, pain on swallowing or ear pain that lasts.
- The hoarseness began after **surgery** or after a **breathing tube**.
- Your job depends on your voice.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is it serious?**
Usually not. Even so, hoarseness lasting more than 4 weeks should always be checked with a camera.

**Will it go away?**
In most cases it improves a lot with voice therapy and changes in habits. If you strain your voice again, it can come back.

**Why do I have it if I do not have nodules?**
Because the problem is the tension with which you use your voice, not a lesion. Nasendoscopy confirms this.

**Can I keep working?**
Usually yes, adapting how you use your voice. We explain how in clinic, and your therapist shows you how to do it.

**Is it caused by reflux?**
It may contribute but is usually not the only cause. We do not give reflux medicine without first looking at the larynx.

**Is it psychological?**
Not necessarily. Stress tightens muscles, but the cause is physical: how the voice is used.
